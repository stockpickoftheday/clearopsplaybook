# Privacy & Data Request Kit

> Ten ready-to-use procedures for handling data requests. Written out in full — no blanks to fill in.

These procedures are general guidance for a small business, not legal advice. Privacy rules differ by place, so check your own local privacy law before you rely on them, and get proper legal advice if a request is complicated or disputed.

## SOP 01 — Taking in a data request

**Purpose:** To make sure every data request is spotted, written down and answered on time, no matter how it arrives — by email, through the website contact form, by phone, in person, or on social media.

**Scope:** This covers every request from a client, former client, email subscriber or website visitor asking about their personal data: what we hold, a copy of it, a correction, or deletion. It applies to Fern & Field Coaching, a 2-person coaching business with a client list, an email list and an online booking system.

**Who does it:** Whoever first sees the request takes it in. One person is named as the privacy contact and owns the request from start to finish.

### Steps

1. Spot the request. Check the inbox, the website contact form, voicemail and social media messages every working day. A request counts even if the person does not use formal words — "what do you have on me?" is a data request.
2. Do not answer the substance straight away. Reply within 2 working days to confirm you received it and say who is handling it.
3. Write it in the request log the same day. Record the date received, the person's name, how they contacted you, and what they asked for in their own words.
4. Start the clock. Mark the deadline on the calendar: most requests must be answered within 30 days of receipt, or sooner if your local law says so. Count from the day the request arrived, not the day you noticed it.
5. Ask the person to confirm what they want if the request is unclear. For example: "Do you want a copy of everything we hold, or only your booking history?"
6. Pass the request to the privacy contact with the log entry number. If the privacy contact is away, the other team member takes over.
7. Tell the person if you need proof of identity from them (see SOP 02).
8. File the original message. Save the email or a screenshot of the message in the request folder, named with the log entry number and the date.

**Quality check:** Before the end of the day, the privacy contact checks the log entry is complete, the deadline is on the calendar, and the person has had an acknowledgement.

**If it goes wrong:** If a request is found late — for example, buried in a spam folder — log it at once with the true date it arrived, tell the person, apologise, and prioritise it. Do not backdate the log or pretend it arrived later than it did.

**Records:** Keep the original message, the log entry, the acknowledgement sent, and any clarification exchanged, in the request folder for at least 2 years after the request is closed.

## SOP 02 — Checking who is asking (identity check)

**Purpose:** To make sure personal data is only given to the person it belongs to, or to someone properly acting for them, and never handed to a stranger by mistake.

**Scope:** This covers every access, deletion or correction request at Fern & Field Coaching where data will be shared, changed or deleted. It applies to clients, former clients and email subscribers.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact runs the identity check, and the other team member never releases data until the privacy contact confirms the check is passed.

### Steps

1. Decide how strong the check needs to be. A request for a copy of all data needs a stronger check than fixing a spelling mistake. When in doubt, use the stronger check.
2. Ask for two matching pieces of information that only the real person would know. Good options are the email address on the account, the phone number on the booking, the date of their last session, or the name of their coach.
3. An email from the same address on file counts as one piece; ask for one more from the list above.
4. If the request came from a different email address, by phone, or in person, ask for two pieces and check them against the client list and booking system first.
5. If someone says they are acting for the person — a parent, a partner, a lawyer — ask for written permission from the person, or proof of legal authority, before sharing anything. A verbal "they said it's fine" is not enough.
6. Record in the log which pieces matched, and the date. Do not store copies of identity documents; if your local law requires a copy, keep it in the request folder only and delete it as soon as the request is closed.
7. If the check fails or the person cannot provide the information, tell them what else they can provide, and do not release, change or delete any data until the check passes.
8. If you suspect a fake request or a trick to get someone else's data, stop, tell the other team member, and log it as a suspected impersonation attempt. Do not tell the requester what information you hold.

**Quality check:** The privacy contact re-checks the log before any data leaves the business: the identity check is marked passed and the person receiving the data is the person it belongs to.

**If it goes wrong:** If data is sent to the wrong person, treat it as a data breach at once and follow SOP 08. Tell the privacy contact immediately, do not try to fix it quietly, and write down exactly what was sent, to whom, and when.

**Records:** Keep a note of the identity check in the request log, including what was checked and the outcome. Delete any copies of identity documents as soon as the request is closed.

## SOP 03 — Finding the person's data

**Purpose:** To find all the personal data a business holds about someone, in every place it might live, so that nothing is missed when answering a request.

**Scope:** This covers every search for one person's data at Fern & Field Coaching: the client list, the email list, the booking system, invoices and payment records, emails, session notes, feedback forms, and any paper files. It applies whenever an access, deletion or correction request is being handled.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact runs the search, with help from the other team member if that person holds the records. One person is responsible for confirming the search is complete.

### Steps

1. List every place personal data could be. For Fern & Field Coaching, the list is: the client spreadsheet, the email list tool, the booking system, the invoicing tool, the email inbox, the shared drive, session notes, feedback forms, and the paper filing box.
2. Search each place using every version of the person's name and email address — including old addresses, nicknames and changed names. Note each place searched and what was found.
3. Check shared places too: group coaching lists, referral notes, and any spreadsheet a client was added to by mistake.
4. Check the inbox and sent mail for the person's name. Include attachments, forwarded messages and old threads.
5. Check backups and archives. If the person appears in a backup, note it. You do not usually need to restore a backup, but you must know it is there.
6. Check with the other team member. Ask whether they hold anything about this person on their own phone, laptop or notebook, and record the answer.
7. Make one list of everything found, grouped by place: what it is, where it lives, and the dates. Mark anything belonging to someone else that must not be shared, such as notes naming another client.
8. Check the list against the request. You only share what the request covers, even if you found more.
9. If you cannot find something you expected, such as a client with sessions but no booking record, note the gap in the log and say so in your answer. Do not pretend the data does not exist.

**Quality check:** Before the answer is prepared, the privacy contact reviews the search list with the other team member and asks whether there is anywhere else this person could appear. The search is only marked complete when both agree.

**If it goes wrong:** If data turns up after the answer was sent, tell the person at once, send it with an apology, and log what was missed and why. If data cannot be found at all, say so honestly and explain the places you searched.

**Records:** Keep the search list — every place searched, what was found, and the date — in the request folder, with the request log entry number on it.

## SOP 04 — Answering an access request

**Purpose:** To give a person a clear, complete copy of the personal data held about them, in a form they can read and use, within the deadline.

**Scope:** This covers every access request (sometimes called a data subject access request) at Fern & Field Coaching: requests for a copy of personal data, or for an explanation of what is held and why. It applies to clients, former clients and email subscribers.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact prepares the answer. The other team member reviews it before it is sent. No access answer goes out without that second pair of eyes.

### Steps

1. Confirm the identity check (SOP 02) is passed and the data search (SOP 03) is complete. Do not start the answer until both are done.
2. Gather the data into one answer pack: contact details, booking history, session notes, invoices and payments, emails to and from them, and email list history. Use the search list from SOP 03 as your checklist.
3. Remove or black out anything belonging to someone else: other clients' names in group session notes, staff comments about other people, and anyone else's contact details. If a note mixes their data with someone else's, share only their part.
4. Write a short cover note in plain English. Say what is included, the dates it covers, why the business holds it, and how long it is normally kept.
5. Choose a safe format. Send it as a password-protected PDF or a secure download link that expires after 7 days, not as a plain email attachment. Give the password by a different route, such as by phone.
6. Send it only to the person, at the address or email verified in the identity check. Double-check the address character by character before sending.
7. Tell the person they can ask questions, ask for corrections, or ask for deletion, and how to do that.
8. Log the date sent, what was included and the format used, and keep a copy of the pack exactly as sent.
9. If the person wants their data in a portable form to take elsewhere, provide a simple file such as a CSV or PDF where that is reasonably possible.

**Quality check:** The second team member reads the pack before it is sent and checks: it is the right person's data, nothing belonging to someone else is in it, and the cover note honestly says what is included and what was left out.

**If it goes wrong:** If the pack goes to the wrong address, treat it as a data breach and follow SOP 08 at once. If something is missing, check the search list, send it with an apology, and log the correction.

**Records:** Keep a copy of the answer pack as sent, the cover note, the date sent and the log entry in the request folder for at least 2 years after the request is closed.

## SOP 05 — Deleting someone's data

**Purpose:** To delete a person's personal data fully and properly when they ask for it, and to confirm the deletion so the person knows it is done.

**Scope:** This covers every deletion request at Fern & Field Coaching: from clients, former clients and email subscribers. It covers the client list, email list, booking system, invoices, emails, session notes, feedback forms and paper files. Some data may have to be kept for legal or tax reasons — see step 5.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact carries out the deletion and the other team member checks it afterwards — deletion is never done by one person alone.

### Steps

1. Confirm the identity check (SOP 02) is passed and the data search (SOP 03) is complete, so you know everything that needs deleting.
2. Check whether any of the data must be kept. Invoices and payment records usually must be kept for tax purposes — check your local tax rules. Tell the person what you must keep, why, and for how long, and keep only the minimum needed.
3. Work through the search list place by place: delete their record from the client list, remove them from the email list, delete or anonymise their booking history, delete their session notes and feedback forms, and delete emails to and from them.
4. Check group coaching lists, referral notes and spreadsheets where their name appears, and remove those entries too.
5. Check the other team member's phone, laptop and notebook. Ask directly and record the answer.
6. Shred paper files — cross shredding is best; do not just throw paper in the bin.
7. Ask any tool or service that holds a copy, such as your booking system or email tool, to delete the data too, and keep the confirmation.
8. Do not delete the request log entry itself. The log is your proof of what you did; remove the person's details from it only if your local law requires that.
9. Within 5 working days of finishing, send written confirmation of what was deleted, what had to be kept and why, and the date.
10. Have the second team member check the deletion by searching the main places again and confirming the person's name no longer appears, then sign off in the log.

**Quality check:** The second team member re-searches the client list, email list, booking system and inbox for the person's name and email. The deletion is only marked complete when no record of them appears, apart from anything legally required to keep.

**If it goes wrong:** If data is found after confirmation was sent, delete it at once, tell the person, and apologise. If data was deleted that should have been kept for tax, stop and take advice before doing anything else. Log everything.

**Records:** Keep the deletion checklist, the confirmation sent, and the second-person check in the request folder for at least 2 years, and note the deletion date in the log.

## SOP 06 — Fixing wrong data

**Purpose:** To correct personal data that is wrong or out of date, quickly and in every place it appears, so the business does not keep using bad information.

**Scope:** This covers every correction request at Fern & Field Coaching: wrong names, email addresses, phone numbers, session dates, or any other personal detail a client or subscriber says is wrong. It applies to the client list, email list, booking system, invoices and session notes.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact makes the correction and the other team member checks it. Small fixes, such as a misspelt first name, are still logged like any other request.

### Steps

1. Log the request the same day it arrives, using SOP 01, and write down exactly what the person says is wrong and what the correct detail is.
2. Confirm the identity check (SOP 02) is passed before changing anything, so you know you are dealing with the right person.
3. Find every place the wrong detail appears, using the search method in SOP 03. A wrong email address may be in the client list, the email list and the booking system at the same time.
4. Check the evidence if the correction is not obvious. For example, ask someone to confirm a different name spelling in writing, and check the booking system and calendar before changing a disputed session date.
5. Fix it in every place you found it, on the same day where possible — not in one place only.
6. If the wrong detail was shared with anyone else, such as an accountant or a venue, tell them about the correction, unless the person who asked says not to.
7. Tell the person the correction is done, list the places that were fixed, and give the date. If you disagree that the data is wrong, explain why and tell them they can add a short note of their own view to the record.
8. If the correction concerns a session note or an opinion rather than a fact, do not rewrite history — add a dated note of the person's view next to the original and keep both.
9. The next day, search for the old detail once more to make sure it is gone from the main systems.

**Quality check:** The second team member checks the corrected record in the client list, email list and booking system, confirms the old detail is gone from all of them, and notes the check in the log.

**If it goes wrong:** If the wrong record or detail is changed, fix it at once, tell the person, and log the mistake. If a correction cannot be made, for example on an invoice already sent to the tax office, explain why and offer to add a note instead.

**Records:** Keep the original request, the evidence, a note of every place changed, and the confirmation sent, in the request folder for at least 2 years.

## SOP 07 — Saying no or asking for more time (refusals and extensions)

**Purpose:** To handle requests a business cannot fully meet — because they are excessive, repeated or need more time — fairly, politely and within the law.

**Scope:** This covers refusals, partial refusals and deadline extensions for data requests at Fern & Field Coaching. It applies when a request is very broad, repeated without good reason, asks for data that must be kept, or needs more than 30 days to answer.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact decides, and the other team member reviews the decision before it is sent — a refusal is never sent by one person alone. If the decision is disputed, take legal advice first.

### Steps

1. Read the request carefully and decide what the problem is: the size, the deadline, a repeat of an answered request, or data that cannot be shared or deleted? Write the reason down before you reply.
2. Check the deadline first. If the request is large or complex, tell the person before the deadline runs out, explain why, and give a new date, normally no more than 30 extra days.
3. Never ignore a request: even if you plan to refuse it, the person must hear from you within the deadline, with a clear reason.
4. If it repeats an answered request and nothing has changed, you may refuse to answer it again — first check the log to confirm what was sent and when.
5. If part of the request can be met and part cannot, meet the part you can. For example, share their booking history even if some session notes cannot be shared because they name other people.
6. Write the reply in plain English, saying what you are doing, what you are not doing, and why. Give the exact reason — "we must keep invoices for tax purposes" is better than "we cannot do that."
7. Tell the person what they can do next: ask you to look again or contact their local privacy regulator, whose name and details you should give.
8. Offer a practical alternative where you can. For example, offer the most recent 12 months now and the rest by the new date.
9. Do not charge a fee for a normal request. Only consider a fee if the request is clearly excessive or repeated and your local law allows it — take advice first.

**Quality check:** The second team member reads every refusal or extension before it is sent and checks the reason is specific, honest and polite, any new date is realistic, and the right to complain is included.

**If it goes wrong:** If the person complains or a regulator contacts you, stop and take legal advice before replying. Log the complaint, keep every message, and delete nothing connected to the request.

**Records:** Keep the letter, the reason in the log, the regulator details, and any follow-up in the request folder for at least 2 years.

## SOP 08 — Handling a data breach

**Purpose:** To act fast when personal data is lost, stolen, shared wrongly or seen by someone who should not see it, so the harm is limited and the right people are told.

**Scope:** This covers any data breach at Fern & Field Coaching: an email sent to the wrong address, a lost laptop or phone, a hacked account, a stolen paper file, or a booking system account that was broken into. It applies to client and subscriber data.

**Who does it:** Whoever spots the breach tells the privacy contact at once. The privacy contact leads the response, with both team members helping — no one tries to fix a breach quietly alone.

### Steps

1. Tell the privacy contact immediately, by phone if needed, not the next working day.
2. Contain it. Change passwords for any account involved, and ask the provider to lock the account if needed. If a laptop or phone is lost, change the passwords for everything on it and wipe it remotely if possible.
3. Work out what happened. Write down what data was involved, whose it was, how many people are affected, how it happened, and when. Write only what you know, and update the note as you learn more.
4. Stop it getting worse. If an email went to the wrong person, ask them to delete it and confirm. If a link was shared wrongly, revoke it. If paper was lost, retrace where it was last seen.
5. Decide who must be told. Check your local privacy law: many places require telling the privacy regulator within 72 hours of a serious breach, and telling those affected if there is a real risk to them. Report within the required time and keep a copy. When in doubt, take advice quickly.
6. Tell the people affected in plain English. Say what happened, what data was involved, what you have done, and what they can do. Apologise honestly.
7. Write a breach record: when it was found, what happened, the data and people involved, what was done, who was told and when, and what will change to stop it recurring.
8. Fix the cause. If a wrong address caused it, add a double-check step for sending client data. If a weak password caused it, set strong passwords and turn on two-step sign-in.

**Quality check:** Within 7 days, both team members review the breach record and check the cause is fixed, everyone who needed telling was told, and the record is complete and honest.

**If it goes wrong:** If a breach is found late, log the true dates it happened and was discovered, tell those affected and the regulator at once, and do not hide the delay. If it is bigger than first thought, update everyone told.

**Records:** Keep the breach record, the regulator report, messages sent to those affected, and any advice received in a separate breach file for at least 3 years, apart from the normal log.

## SOP 09 — Keeping a request log

**Purpose:** To keep one complete, honest record of every data request and what happened to it, so the business can prove it answered on time and learn from any mistakes.

**Scope:** This covers the request log: every access, deletion, correction and breach-related request, from the day it arrives until it is closed. The log is kept by both team members and checked monthly.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact owns the log. Whoever takes in a request adds the first entry the same day, and the other team member checks the log at the end of each week.

### Steps

1. Use one log only. Keep a single spreadsheet or notebook for all requests, named clearly (for example, "Data Request Log") in the shared drive where both team members can reach it.
2. Give every request a number, starting at 001 each year (for example, 2026-001), and use it on every file, email and note for that request.
3. Record the basics on the day the request arrives: date received, the person's name, how they contacted you, what they asked for, and who is handling it.
4. Add to the entry as work happens: when the identity check passed, the search was done, the answer was sent, and the request was closed.
5. Record the deadline and whether it was met; if it was missed, write the reason honestly, for example "found in spam folder 5 days late."
6. Record refusals, extensions and complaints in the same log, with the reason and the date the person was told.
7. Keep entries short and factual — what happened, not opinions about the person. Never write anything you would not want the person to read.
8. Check the log every Friday for requests with no update for 7 days, requests near their deadline, and entries missing dates. Fix anything found the same day.
9. Once a month, the privacy contact reviews the whole log — how many requests came in, how many met the deadline, whether any type keeps causing trouble — and adds one short note of what was learned.
10. Keep the log safe: it holds people's names and request details, so protect it with a password and do not share it outside the business.

**Quality check:** At the monthly review, the privacy contact checks every request has a closing date or a clear next step and none is missing a deadline. Any gap is fixed before the review is signed off.

**If it goes wrong:** If an entry is wrong, correct it with a dated note showing the change — do not delete it or rewrite it silently. If the log file is lost or damaged, rebuild it from the request folders and emails, and note that it was rebuilt.

**Records:** Keep the log for at least 2 years after its last entry is closed, password-protected and backed up with the rest of the business files.

## SOP 10 — Training staff on privacy basics

**Purpose:** To make sure everyone who handles personal data knows the basics: what it is, how to keep it safe, and what to do when someone makes a data request.

**Scope:** This covers training for both team members, plus anyone who helps the business, such as a temporary assistant, a virtual assistant or a new coach. It covers day-to-day handling of client data, not legal training.

**Who does it:** The privacy contact runs the training, both team members attend every session, and a new helper is trained before getting access to any client data.

### Steps

1. Train every new person before they touch client data: show them where it lives (client list, email list, booking system), what they may and may not do with it, and who the privacy contact is.
2. Cover the basics: personal data is any information about a real person — names, email addresses, phone numbers, booking history, session notes, payment details. Treat it as if it belonged to you.
3. Teach the daily habits: lock your screen when you leave your desk, use strong passwords and two-step sign-in, never send client data to a personal email, and never leave paper files where visitors can see them.
4. Teach the request routine: any data request goes to the privacy contact the same day, and no data is shared, changed or deleted until the identity check (SOP 02) is passed. Practise with a made-up example.
5. Teach the breach routine: if something goes wrong — a wrong email, a lost phone, a strange login — tell the privacy contact at once. There is no blame for reporting quickly.
6. Run a short refresher every 6 months with one real (anonymised) example from the request log, and update the training for any new tools or data types.
7. Check understanding, don't just talk: at the end of each session, ask each person to explain in their own words what they would do if a client asked for deletion. If they cannot, go through it again.
8. If a helper only does one job, such as sending the newsletter, train them on that job only — but still tell them who the privacy contact is and what to do if a subscriber asks about their data.

**Quality check:** After every session, the privacy contact writes a short note of who attended, what was covered, and whether each person could explain the request and breach routines. Everyone who attended signs it.

**If it goes wrong:** If someone shares, changes or deletes data without following the SOPs, fix it first (see SOP 08 if data went to the wrong person), then retrain that person one-to-one and log the incident. Repeated mistakes mean the process needs to change, not just the person.

**Records:** Keep a training record for each person — date, what was covered, who attended — in the shared drive for at least 2 years after they leave.
