SOPs and Checklists: What's the Difference, and Do You Need Both?

SOPs and checklists are often confused, and many small businesses use one when they really need the other. The short version: an SOP explains how to do a task, and a checklist confirms that each step was done. They are partners, not rivals — and most businesses need both.

What an SOP does

An SOP is the full instruction for a task. It explains the steps in order, says who is responsible, lists the tools or supplies needed, and notes what to do when something goes wrong. A new starter reads an SOP to learn how a job works from scratch. SOPs are the reference you turn to when you do not know how to do something, or when you need to check the detail of a step. They can be a page or several pages long, because explaining a job properly takes space.

What a checklist does

A checklist is a short list of items to tick off as they are done. It assumes the person already knows how to do the job — it only helps them remember every step, in the right order, without skipping one. A pilot uses a checklist before takeoff not because they do not know how to fly, but because skipping one item can have serious results. In a small business, checklists work the same way for opening, closing, cleaning, and safety checks: quick, easy to follow, and hard to get wrong.

How they work together

The best setup is simple: the SOP teaches the task, and the checklist makes sure it is done right every time. A new starter learns closing from the SOP, then uses the closing checklist each evening once they know the steps. If they forget a detail, the SOP is there to check. This pairing also keeps both documents short: the SOP holds the explanations, so the checklist can stay to one page of short items. When a process changes, update the SOP first, then check whether the checklist needs the same change.

When a checklist alone is enough

Some jobs are simple enough that a checklist is all you need. If the task has few steps, everyone on the team already knows how to do it, and the only risk is forgetting a step, a checklist does the job. Daily cleaning routines, stock counts, and end-of-day lock-ups are common examples. The test is simple: if a new starter could not do the job from the checklist alone, you also need an SOP. If they could, the checklist on its own is fine, and writing a full SOP would only add paperwork nobody reads.

When you need the full SOP

You need an SOP when the job is new to the person doing it, when the steps are complicated or must happen in a strict order, or when a mistake has real consequences. Safety procedures, handling money, dealing with complaints, and operating machinery all fall into this group. You also need SOPs when a task is done rarely: if someone only does a job once a month, they will not remember the details, and a checklist of short reminders will not be enough. In these cases, write the SOP first, then pull a checklist out of it for everyday use.

Frequently asked questions

Can I turn an SOP into a checklist?

Yes, and you should. Take the steps from the SOP, shorten each one to a few words, and put them in a tick-off list. Keep the SOP as the reference for anyone who needs the detail.

How long should a checklist be?

Short enough to use at a glance — usually one page. If a checklist grows past a page, split the task or move the detail back into the SOP.

Should staff sign checklists?

For safety, money, and closing routines, yes. A signed and dated checklist shows the task was done and who did it, which helps when something goes wrong later.

Do checklists replace training?

No. A checklist helps a trained person remember the steps. Training — usually with the SOP — is what teaches them the job in the first place.
