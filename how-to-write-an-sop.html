How to Write an SOP Your Team Will Actually Follow

Most SOPs fail for a simple reason: they are written for the manager, not for the person doing the job. A useful SOP reads like clear instructions from a helpful colleague — short sentences, steps in order, and nothing extra. Follow the process below and your team will actually use what you write.

Step 1: Pick one task and watch it happen

Choose a single task with a clear start and a clear finish, such as closing the shop or handling a return. Then watch the best person on your team do it, and write down every step exactly as it happens — including the small steps they do without thinking. Those automatic habits are usually the ones a new person misses. If you write from memory instead, you will leave gaps, and the SOP will not work for someone who has never done the job before.

Step 2: Write the steps in plain language

Write each step as one short action, starting with a verb: open, check, record, call, lock. Put the steps in the order they happen, and number them so the order is obvious. Use the words your team already uses, not formal or technical language, and keep sentences short enough to read at a glance. If a step has a choice in it — for example, what to do when a customer pays by card versus cash — split it into two steps or add a short note under the step. When in doubt, make it simpler rather than more complete.

Step 3: Add the details that prevent mistakes

A list of steps is a good start, but a few extra details turn it into a reliable SOP. Add these where they matter:

  • Who is responsible for the task, and who covers it when they are away.
  • What tools, supplies, or passwords are needed before starting.
  • What "done" looks like, so the person knows when to stop.
  • The common mistakes to avoid, and what to do if something goes wrong.
  • Any safety, legal, or customer-service points that must not be skipped.

Keep these notes short. A warning buried in a long paragraph will be missed; a single clear line will be remembered.

Step 4: Test it with someone who has never done the job

This is the step most businesses skip, and it is the one that matters most. Hand the draft to someone who has not done the task and ask them to follow it exactly, without asking questions. Watch where they pause, guess, or go wrong — every pause is a place where the SOP needs a clearer step. Fix those spots, then test again if the task is important or risky. A tested SOP is one you can trust; an untested one is only a guess about how the job works.

Step 5: Keep it where the work happens

An SOP only helps if people can find it in the moment they need it. Store SOPs in one shared place everyone knows about, and put printed copies near the work itself when it makes sense — by the till, in the kitchen, or on the workshop wall. Tell the team when a new SOP is ready and walk through it once together. Then treat it as a living document: when the process changes, update the SOP the same week, and note the date of the change at the top so everyone knows which version is current.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to write one SOP?

A simple everyday SOP often takes under an hour from watching the task to a finished draft. A longer or safety-related one takes more time, mainly because testing it properly matters more.

Should SOPs include pictures or screenshots?

Yes, when a step is hard to describe in words. A photo of the right switch, screen, or setup removes doubt and helps new staff far more than another paragraph.

What if my team ignores the SOPs?

Ask why before adding more rules. Often the SOP is too long, too hard to find, or out of date. Shorten it, move it closer to the work, and involve the team in fixing it.

Can I start from a template instead of a blank page?

Yes. A ready-made pack gives you a tested structure and wording to adapt, which is much faster than starting from scratch — just change the details to match your business.
