Maintenance

How Often Should You Update Your SOPs?

An SOP is only useful while it matches how the work is actually done. The moment a tool changes, a supplier changes, or the team finds a better way, the written procedure starts going out of date. The answer is not to rewrite everything constantly, but to review on a schedule and update straight away when something changes.

Review every SOP at least once a year

As a baseline, read through each SOP once a year and check that every step still matches reality. Pick a quiet month and work through the set a few at a time, asking the people who do each job whether anything has changed. A yearly review catches the slow drift that nobody notices day to day: a step everyone skips, a tool that was replaced, or a form that no longer exists. Note the review date on each SOP, even when nothing needed changing, so the team can see it was checked.

Update immediately when something changes

Some changes cannot wait for the yearly review. Update the SOP straight away when any of these happen:

  • A tool, machine, supplier, or piece of software changes.
  • A rule or regulation affecting the task changes.
  • The team finds a better or safer way to do the job.
  • A mistake happens because the SOP was unclear or wrong.
  • A new product, service, or location is added to the business.

Make the change the same week, tell the team, and replace old printed copies. An out-of-date SOP is worse than no SOP, because it tells people to do the wrong thing with confidence.

Check safety and money SOPs more often

Not every SOP needs the same attention. Safety procedures, food handling, childcare routines, and anything involving money or customer data deserve a check every few months, because a mistake there costs the most. Everyday routines such as opening and ordering can usually wait for the yearly review unless something changes. Sorting your SOPs into these two groups keeps the review workload small and focuses effort where the risk is highest.

Use mistakes and questions as update signals

The best sign that an SOP needs updating is not the calendar — it is what happens on the floor. If the same question keeps being asked, the SOP probably does not answer it. If the same mistake keeps happening, the SOP is probably unclear or wrong. If new starters keep getting stuck at the same step, that step needs rewriting. Treat every repeated question or error as a prompt to open the SOP and fix it, rather than as a fault in the person asking.

Keep the update routine simple

A review system only works if it is easy to follow. Keep a simple list of every SOP with its last review date and the name of the person responsible for it. When you update an SOP, change the version date at the top, save it in the same shared place, and tell the team what changed in one or two sentences. Remove or clearly mark old printed copies so nobody follows an outdated version by accident. Small, regular updates beat a big rewrite every few years — and they keep your SOPs trusted instead of ignored.

Frequently asked questions

What if nothing has changed — do I still review?

Yes, briefly. A quick read-through confirms the SOP is still right and gives the team a chance to suggest improvements. Mark the review date and move on.

Who should be responsible for updates?

The owner or manager owns the schedule, but the person who does the job should check the detail. They are the first to know when reality and the SOP no longer match.

Should I keep old versions of an SOP?

Keep a short record of what changed and when, especially for safety and quality procedures. You do not need to keep every old draft, but a simple change note is useful.

How do I get staff to follow the updated SOP?

Tell them what changed and why, in person or in a short note. People follow updates they understand; a silent file change in a shared folder gets missed.
